Executive summary

Chinese construction, material-handling and industrial-equipment makers have taken large shares of India's and Southeast Asia's most contested equipment categories. They did it mainly by redesigning the commercial system around the machine, not by selling it cheaply. Most of their moves can be copied, and several are cheaper for incumbents to make than they were for the Chinese firms that made them first.

The shift is large. Between 2019 and 2024 China's share of Indonesia's excavator imports rose from 33% to 75% while Japan's fell from 24% to 7%, and China's share of India's crane imports rose from 35% to 93%.7,9 By 2024 China supplied about two-thirds of forklift imports in India, Indonesia, Thailand and Malaysia.10–13 The leading Chinese makers now earn around half or more of their revenue abroad; SANY earns 63%.1

We tested whether this is a commodity cycle, and it is not. In Indonesia, Komatsu sold about 750 fewer machines in 2025 than in 2023, in a market we estimate grew by about 4,400 units while coal output was at or near a record.15,17–19 Coal, nickel and highway cycles amplified Chinese gains but did not cause them. By our tests the shift is structural in India and Indonesia, holds in four of five categories in Thailand, and remains contestable where uptime and resale value decide the purchase (Chapter 3).

Six learnings explain most of the gains:

  1. Enter through a narrow beachhead, then broaden. SANY India took 52% of the long-reach excavator niche within about 18 months, then used the network it built to sell cranes, piling rigs and port machinery.21,22
  2. Treat customer credit as part of the product. India's trade regulator found Chinese exporters giving related importers up to three years' credit,14 and in September 2026 Indonesia licensed SANY's own finance company.23
  3. Price just enough, then invest in the lifecycle. Chinese cranes undercut Indian prices by only 0 to 10% in 2023–24.14 Chinese firms are now building parts and remanufacturing capacity, the business that earned United Tractors 37% of its machinery revenue in the first half of 2026.24,89
  4. Localise plants, then export from the host. Four Chinese OEMs build in India, and five more Chinese plants and service bases have opened or broken ground in Indonesia and Thailand since November 2025.21,25–32
  5. Turn local-content and investment rules into an advantage. Indonesia now gives a 25% local-content credit for investing locally,33 and India promises a 60-day decision on minority Chinese investment in capital goods.34
  6. Use electrification as the entry wedge. China supplied 60 to 69% of electric-forklift imports in India, Indonesia and Thailand in 2024.10–12

The playbook has limits. Tata Hitachi won back mining-excavator share, from 20% to about 35%; Japanese brands held Thai excavators; and Neta's collapse in Thailand showed what happens when a dealer network outgrows its parts supply.42,38,46

What this means for each group of readers:

  • Global incumbents already own the finance arms, installed bases and local plants that Chinese firms spent a decade building. The priority is to aim them at first-time buyers and to publish evidence on uptime and resale value.
  • Indian and ASEAN champions can use India's Press Note 2 and Indonesia's local-content rules to take Chinese technology or capital on their own terms.
  • Dealers should choose brands niche by niche and compete on parts depth and service response, where Chinese networks are still thinner.
  • Financiers now hold Chinese-brand credit as a mainstream exposure and need brand-level residual-value evidence to price it.
  • Chinese OEMs planning their next market should expect trade remedies once their share is high, and build parts depth before dealer count.
  • Policymakers in India and Indonesia are choosing between Chinese capital that builds locally and Chinese machines that arrive as imports. Their current rules lean towards capital.

Chapter 1. Why learn from Chinese OEMs now

In briefThe leading Chinese equipment makers became majority-overseas businesses in 2025 and 2026, in the same months that trade and investment rules in India, Indonesia and the United States reset. For firms setting their 2027–2030 plans for Asia, the Chinese playbook is now the benchmark to plan against.

Chinese OEMs now earn half or more of their revenue abroad

For two decades Chinese construction-machinery makers grew on their home market and exported what it could not absorb. That is no longer true. In 2025 SANY earned 63% of its revenue outside China, Zoomlion 58.56% and XCMG 48.2%, and XCMG passed half in the first half of 2026.1,3,4,5 LiuGong earned 47.65%, and the two largest forklift makers, Heli and Hangcha, 44% and 43.38%.6,36,47

Exhibit 4 Overseas revenue of Chinese industrial OEMs, FY2025Pack 2 · position 6 · not picked: Built 4

Overseas business also carries the margin. SANY's gross margin abroad was 31.7% in 2025, two points higher than a year earlier.1 LiuGong calls internationalisation essential to its survival and aims to take overseas revenue above half during China's 15th Five-Year Plan.6

A collapse at home pushed them abroad

The turn outwards was forced as much as chosen. China's domestic excavator sales fell from a record 292,864 units in 2020 to 89,980 in 2023, a drop of almost 70%, while exports tripled to 105,038.49,50 In 2023 Chinese makers sold more excavators abroad than at home for the first time. By 2025 the two were almost level, at 118,518 at home and 116,739 abroad, and in the first half of 2026 exports grew a further 33.5%.51–53

Indonesia matters most to this shift. It was China's largest excavator export market in 2024, taking US$882m of machines, ahead of Russia.41

The second layer: finance, aftermarket and plants

The newest Chinese moves are about what surrounds the machine. Between June and September 2026 Chinese makers opened or started plants in Thailand and Indonesia, XCMG broke ground on its first overseas remanufacturing base, and Indonesia licensed SANY's own finance company.23,24,29–31 Finance, service and local production are where incumbents have earned their margins and their customers' loyalty. Chinese firms are now investing in all three, as Chapters 5 to 7 show.

The rules reset in the same window

At least nine policy events in 2026 changed how equipment is made, sold and financed in the region. Together they push towards local production and away from exporting from China.

Exhibit 6 Six policy events in 2026 opened access and three raised barriersBuilt 5 · position 7 · not picked: Pack 3
  • India made the largest moves. In January it withdrew a planned certification rule for pumps, compressors, cranes and construction equipment.57 In March, Press Note 2 created a 60-day decision track for minority investment in capital goods from China and other countries that share a land border with India (Chapter 8).34,35 India also held back. The duties its trade regulator recommended on Chinese cranes in September 2025 were not imposed within the three months its rules allow, and were still not in force in August 2026.37,new
  • Trade agreements changed access. The UK–India agreement entered into force on 15 July, and the EU–India agreement was sent to the Council for signature on 11 September.58,60 The US–Indonesia agreement of 19 February cut the US tariff on many Indonesian goods to 19% and exempted US products from Indonesia's local-content rules.61
  • US policy became a moving target for any plant that exports. After the Supreme Court struck down tariffs imposed under emergency powers in February, a 10% surcharge replaced them until 24 July.62–64 In March the US opened an investigation into excess capacity in 16 economies, including all five in this paper; it had produced no tariffs by August.65,66 A separate Section 301 action, on the enforcement of bans on imports made with forced labour, has set tariffs since 24 July: 10% on goods from India, Indonesia and Malaysia, and 12.5% on goods from Thailand, Vietnam and China.67

The question for this paper

For firms now setting their 2027–2030 plans, three planning assumptions follow. Chinese competitors treat overseas growth as a necessity. They are building the finance and aftermarket layers where incumbents earn their margins. And policy in India and Indonesia is moving from keeping Chinese firms out to channelling Chinese capital into local production.

The question for industrial leaders is therefore no longer whether Chinese OEMs are serious competitors in India and Southeast Asia. It is which of the moves that won them share can be copied, by whom, and how fast. Chapter 2 maps where share has moved, Chapter 3 tests whether the shift will last, and Chapters 4 to 9 set out the six moves.

Chapter 2. Where the shift is happening

In briefChinese gains are broad but uneven. China supplies almost all of India's crane imports, three-quarters of Indonesia's excavator imports and about two-thirds of forklift imports across the region, but it has made no headway in Thai excavators.

This chapter uses the one consistent public dataset across all five countries: the value of imports by country of origin, as reported to UN Comtrade. Import data record where a machine was shipped from, not whose badge it carries, and they miss machines built locally. Appendix A sets out these limits.

China's share of imports rose almost everywhere

Between 2019 and 2024, China's share of import value rose in 18 of the 19 country–category pairs with data, and it now exceeds half in 12. Three patterns stand out.

Exhibit 7 China's share of import value by country and category, 2019 → 2024 (cell colour shows the 2024 share)Pack 4 · position 8 · not picked: Built 6
  • Forklifts are China's most consistent gain. China supplies about two-thirds or more of forklift imports in every country with 2024 data, up from 32–53% in 2019. The switch to electric forklifts explains much of this (Chapter 9).
  • Cranes show the starkest change. China's share of India's self-propelled crane imports rose from 35% to 93% while their value more than quadrupled, to US$623m.9 Chapter 10 examines how.
  • Excavators split the region. China now supplies three-quarters of Indonesia's excavator imports, half of Malaysia's and a third of India's, but its share of Thai imports barely moved (Chapter 12).

In compressors and pumps China was already the largest supplier in most of these markets in 2019, and it gained further; in Thai compressors it overtook Japan.68 The one fall is in Indian pumps, where China's share was flat at about 35%, consistent with a strong domestic pump industry.70

Indian figures need a caution. More than 95% of the construction equipment sold in India is made there, so import shares describe a small slice of the market.77 They also miss the machines Chinese makers build in their Indian plants (Chapter 7), and so understate the Chinese presence.

Japanese and Korean suppliers lost the most

Excavators, the largest category by value, allow a direct comparison. Japan's share of imports fell from 36.1% to 23.7% in India and from 23.9% to 6.7% in Indonesia, and held at about 18% in Malaysia. It rose in Thailand, from 39.8% to 50.8%, and in Vietnam, from 41.7% to 50.5% between 2019 and 2023.7,8,38,40,75 China gained where import demand grew and stood still where it was flat: import value almost trebled in India and doubled in Indonesia and Malaysia, but was flat in Thailand.

Exhibit 8 China's and Japan's shares of excavator import value, 2019 → 2024, with Korea and totals in the tablePack 5 · position 9

Measured against their starting shares, Korea's losses were steeper than Japan's in every market. In Vietnam its share halved, to 21.4%.75 Vietnam's 2019 figures show Japan and Korea with identical unit counts, which may be a reporting quirk; on a 2020 base the pattern is the same.75 Regional trade blurs the picture slightly, since some of what Indonesia and Malaysia import from Thailand is likely to be Japanese-brand machines built there. Counting that trade as Japanese would raise Japan's figures but not change their direction.7,40

Vietnam has not yet reported its 2024 data, but exporters' figures fill the gap. In 2024 China reported US$189.4m of excavator exports to Vietnam, Japan US$161.1m and Korea US$59.5m, which gives China about 46% of the three countries' shipments.41 The two measures are not strictly comparable, but the direction is clear.

In Indonesia, company disclosures confirm the trade data

Indonesia is the one market in scope where incumbent brand shares are published regularly, because the distributors of both leading Japanese brands are listed. Komatsu's share, as reported by United Tractors, fell from 29% in 2023 to 20% in 2025 and 17% in the first four months of 2026.15,17,81 Dividing its unit sales by its share gives a rough size for the market: about 18,200 units in 2023 and 22,600 in 2025. On that basis Komatsu sold about 750 fewer machines in a market that grew by about 4,400.

Exhibit 9 Komatsu and Hitachi market shares in Indonesia, and the implied market size, 2019–2026Pack 6 · position 10 · not picked: Built 7

Hitachi's share, as reported by its distributor Hexindo, followed a similar path.82,83 The two series measure different things, so their sum is only indicative. On that basis the two Japanese brands held about 50% of the market in 2023 and about 37% in 2025, and the difference went mainly to Chinese brands, SANY in particular.82,84

Categories follow a common sequence

Across the region, Chinese internationalisation follows a sequence by category. Construction equipment is the most mature: its leaders are majority-overseas and are building finance, remanufacturing and plants abroad. Material handling is scaling fast, with the first overseas forklift plants opened in 2025 and 2026. Compressors and pumps are still import-led, with little public evidence yet of local channels or plants. Robotics is at the earliest stage: Chinese suppliers held 55% of their home market in 2025, but their business abroad remains small.85,86

The sequence is useful because it predicts what comes next. A material-handling incumbent should expect finance, aftermarket and plants to reach its category within a few years, and a compressor or pump incumbent should expect channel building to follow the import surge. First, though, one question has to be settled: whether these shifts reflect a better commercial system or a commodity cycle that happened to favour Chinese suppliers.

Chapter 3. Structural or cyclical?

In briefCoal, nickel and highway cycles amplified Chinese gains but did not cause them. In India and Indonesia the shift passes the first three tests outright, holds in a control market for four of five categories, and remains contestable where uptime and resale value decide the purchase.

A sceptical reader will ask whether Komatsu lost share in Indonesia because Chinese firms had a better commercial system, or because coal miners, Komatsu's most important customers, stopped buying. The answer decides strategy. A cyclical loss can be waited out; a structural one cannot.

Five tests

We apply five tests. The first asks whether the incumbent loses share in good years as well as bad, the second whether the shift began before the cyclical shock, and the third whether it appears outside the cyclical segment, which here means mining. A yes on any of these points to a structural shift. The fourth compares a similar market without the cycle: a yes supports a structural reading, and a no points to an effect specific to the category. The fifth asks whether lost share has come back anywhere; a yes means the market is still contestable. We then grade each country on a five-point scale: structural, likely structural, inconclusive, likely cyclical or cyclical.

Indonesia: share fell in a growing market at the top of the coal cycle

  • Test 1: good years. Komatsu's share fell from 29% to 20% between 2023 and 2025, while Indonesia mined a record 836 million tonnes of coal in 2024 and 817 million in 2025, and the market we estimate grew by about a third.15,17–19 Hexindo's figures point the same way: its unit sales rose 22% while its share barely moved.83
  • Test 2: timing. China's share of excavator imports rose from 33% to 75% between 2019 and 2024, before the 2026 cut in coal production quotas, which the energy ministry expects to reduce output by 12%, to about 720 million tonnes.7,88 The cut does explain the latest fall: in January to August 2026, United Tractors' Komatsu sales fell 21% to 2,689 units, and its sales of large mining machines fell 49%.89,90
  • Test 3: outside mining. China's share also rose in forklifts, cranes, compressors and pumps, none of which depends mainly on mining.11,72–74 Hexindo, whose largest segment is agribusiness, lost share too.83
  • Test 4: control market. Thailand has no coal or nickel boom, yet China gained there in four of five categories. It did not gain in excavators (Chapter 12).
  • Test 5: reversal. Share has come back before. Komatsu's rose from 21% in 2021 to 29% in 2023, and Hitachi's from 15.8% to 21%.15,79,82

Nickel is often offered as the explanation. Indonesia's policy of processing nickel at home created a new group of customers, many of them Chinese-owned, and SANY's gains have been linked to them.84 The first electric loaders from XCMG's Weda Bay plant went to Tsingshan.30 The effect is real, but it cannot explain Chinese gains in forklifts, pumps or plantation machinery. Nickel amplified the shift; it did not cause it.

India: Chinese gains were made in a boom

  • Test 1: good years. Construction-equipment sales rose 26% in FY24 and reached 140,191 units in FY25, before slipping 2% in FY26.20,93 Over those years China's share of excavator imports rose from 7.7% to 33.9%, and Chinese crane imports from 163 to 938 units a year.8,14
  • Test 2: timing. The FY26 slowdown has clear cyclical causes: highway construction slowed from 36.8 km a day in FY21 to 25 km a day in FY26, and new highway awards fell by almost half from their FY22 peak.94 The import shift began well before it.
  • Test 3: outside the cycle. China's share also rose in forklifts, from 32.5% to 67.6%, and in compressors, from 44.0% to 52.6%.10,71
  • Test 4: control market. Thailand confirms the pattern in four categories of five, as it does for Indonesia.
  • Test 5: reversal. Tata Hitachi's share of mining excavators fell from about 50% to 20% as Chinese makers entered, and has since recovered to about 35%.42

What about state support?

State support is part of the picture. India's trade regulator calculated dumping margins of 30% to 110% on Chinese cranes, and the EU has imposed anti-subsidy duties on Chinese mobile access equipment.14,96 The collapse of China's home market also left the industry with capacity to fill.50 Subsidies and spare capacity explain why Chinese firms could afford to push abroad. They do not explain how they won customers once there. The moves set out in Part II are commercial choices that other firms can observe and, in most cases, copy.

Chapter 4. Learning 1: Enter through a narrow beachhead, then broaden

In briefChinese OEMs did not attack incumbents' core products first. They won an under-served niche, built dealers and service around it, and then used that network to sell the full range.

What Chinese OEMs did

SANY opened its plant at Chakan, near Pune, in 2007, its first outside China, but it did not start in the core of India's excavator market. It went after long-reach excavators, a specialist machine used to clean canals, dig wells and build metro lines. Within about 18 months it had 52% of that niche nationally and 62% in Maharashtra, figures it announced in October 2016.21

The niche mattered less for its size than for what it forced SANY to build: dealers, service engineers and parts depots, which could then sell other products. By 2025 SANY India claimed 60% of India's piling rigs, 55% of its cranes, 50% of port machinery and 20% of excavators.22 These are company claims, but trade data and India's crane case support their direction. In September 2026 SANY India reported about 42 dealers and more than 45,000 machines delivered across India and South Asia.98

In Indonesia the beachhead was a customer group rather than a product. The fastest Chinese growth came from companies building nickel smelters and mines, many of them Chinese-owned, and Hitachi's distributor dates aggressive Chinese competition to 2021, as nickel investment was accelerating.82,84 XCMG now reports more than 30,000 of its machines working in Indonesia.30

Case: cranes in India

The crane market shows the approach applied to a whole category. Chinese crawler and truck crane imports rose from 163 units in FY21 to 938 in FY24, and China supplied virtually all of India's imports. Media reports say five Indian producers have stopped making these cranes.14,37 Chapter 10 sets out how.

Why the beachhead works

Three features recur. The niche sits outside the incumbents' core, so no loyal supplier defends it. It forces the entrant to build the channel it will need later, in particular regions. And it becomes a reference: a 52% share in a visible segment, or a fleet on a prominent industrial park, helps sell the next category.

How incumbents have responded

United Tractors, Komatsu's Indonesian distributor, sells a cheaper second-tier product alongside Komatsu machines and includes up to three years of preventive maintenance with every unit.84 Hexindo will adopt Hitachi Construction Machinery's new LANDCROS brand from April 2027 and widen its range.83 Volvo Construction Equipment went the other way: in June 2025 it sold its 70% stake in SDLG, its value brand, for SEK8bn, to refocus on premium machines for mining, quarrying and heavy infrastructure.100 In India it will keep assembling SDLG machines until the end of 2026 and launch localised Volvo wheel loaders in 2027.101 Every incumbent faces the same choice: meet the challenger with a cheaper line of its own, or retreat to segments where uptime and service still decide the purchase.

Where it stops working

A beachhead is hardest to hold where machines work hard and resale value matters. Tata Hitachi's share of Indian mining excavators fell from about 50% to 20% as Chinese makers entered, then recovered to about 35%, even as the number of competitors doubled to about eight (Chapter 13).42

What transfers

  • Global incumbents should audit the segments where their own range is thin, because that is where the next entrant will land.
  • Indian and ASEAN champions and Chinese OEMs can choose a niche for the channel it builds, not only for its size.
  • Dealers can add a challenger brand in the niche without undercutting their core franchise.

Chapter 5. Learning 2: Treat customer credit as part of the product

In briefFinancing, more than price, gave Chinese OEMs their sharpest advantage. In under a decade they moved from credit between related companies and lender partnerships to owning licensed finance companies.

What Chinese OEMs did

Many equipment buyers in India and Indonesia are small contractors and miners who struggle to borrow from banks. For them the down payment and the monthly instalment decide the purchase more than the list price. Tata Hitachi's managing director said in September 2026 that first-time buyers in road construction were turning to non-bank lenders, and that Chinese makers had gained ground with easier financing.42

In India, the trade regulator found that Chinese crane exporters gave related importers credit of up to three years, which it called “not normal”; XCMG's submission was rejected partly because it misreported its credit terms.14 Long credit between related companies lets an importing subsidiary offer generous terms without borrowing from Indian banks. SANY India also runs its own online auction portal for used machines, run by its customer-finance team.102

Case: SANY in Indonesia, from partner to owner in nine years

SANY's distributor signed a financing agreement with MNC Leasing in December 2017, and the two launched low-down-payment financing a year later.103,104 In September 2025 SANY signed an agreement worth about RMB1.45bn with two finance companies to support customer financing.106 On 14 September 2026 Indonesia's financial regulator licensed PT Sany Indonesia Finance.23 Five days earlier, XCMG and its dealer had launched ABC Multifinance, a joint venture offering finance across the purchase, maintenance, use and eventual transfer of a machine.56,110

One detail shows how far lenders' views have moved. One of SANY's 2025 partners, PT Chandra Sakti Utama Leasing, was acquired in 1995 by the Caterpillar dealer in Indonesia, Trakindo.108,109 When a lender in the Caterpillar dealer's own group finances SANY machines, local lenders plainly regard Chinese-brand credit risk as acceptable.

How incumbents have responded

Incumbents own finance arms; Hexindo lists attractive financing through Hexa Finance as one of its three responses to Chinese competition.82 But the public record shows little evidence of incumbents aiming their finance at the first-time and small-fleet buyers Chinese OEMs have targeted.

Where it stops working

Credit-led share is exposed in a downturn. The 2026 coal-quota cut in Indonesia and payment delays in Indian road building will test customers who bought on easy terms (Chapter 3). Opaque credit between related companies also invites trade remedies, as India's crane case shows.14

What transfers

  • Global incumbents with a captive finance company can copy this move most cheaply: design the down payment, tenor, seasonal repayment and trade-in terms with the machine, and aim them at first-time buyers.
  • Financiers now hold Chinese-brand credit as a mainstream exposure and need residual-value evidence by brand to price it.
  • Indian and ASEAN champions can set up brand finance programmes with non-bank lenders and banks.
  • Chinese OEMs should keep credit terms between related companies transparent.

Chapter 6. Learning 3: Price just enough, then invest in the lifecycle

In briefThe price gap between Chinese and incumbent machines is smaller than commonly assumed. Chinese OEMs priced just low enough to matter and are now investing in parts, service and remanufacturing, the business that earned United Tractors more than a third of its machinery revenue in the first half of 2026 while machine sales fell.

What Chinese OEMs did

The only regulator-verified price comparison in the five countries found that Chinese cranes undercut Indian prices by only 0 to 10% in 2023–24; the damage came from a squeeze on the Indian producer's costs and from long credit (Chapter 10).14 Retail listings point the same way. In India, SANY's 21–22.5 tonne excavators were listed at about INR 41 lakh to INR 61 lakh in September 2026, inside the ranges for Komatsu (INR 41 lakh to INR 76 lakh), JCB (INR 57 lakh to INR 65 lakh) and Tata Hitachi (INR 61 lakh to INR 67 lakh).112–116 On Indonesia's public e-catalogue, median asking prices for 20–22 tonne excavators were Rp1.75bn for SANY and Rp1.80bn for XCMG, against Rp1.87bn for Komatsu, Rp1.90bn for Hitachi and about Rp2.2bn for Caterpillar and Kobelco.117 These are asking prices, not transaction prices, but there is no separate cheap band.

Case: XCMG remanufacturing in Balikpapan

On 16 September 2026 XCMG broke ground on its first overseas base for overhauling and remanufacturing mining-equipment components, combining repair, regional parts stocks, technician training and refurbishment of used machines.24 Its aftermarket revenue grew 33.23% in the first half of 2025, against 8.04% for the company overall, and it reports more than 2,000 service and parts outlets worldwide.5,119

Where it stops working

Resale value is unresolved. Tata Hitachi's managing director has said customers find Chinese machines have “no resale price” and higher failure rates.42 These are an incumbent's claims, and no free public dataset of used prices by brand exists to test them.

What transfers

  • Global incumbents should treat parts, service and remanufacturing as a revenue line to build, and publish uptime and resale evidence.
  • Financiers: whoever first publishes credible residual values by brand is likely to shape lending terms.
  • Dealers can sell parts, service and trade-in as one bundle.
  • Chinese OEMs should invest in parts and remanufacturing early, where the installed base is largest.

Chapter 7. Learning 4: Localise plants, then export from the host

In briefChinese OEMs have built local plants to secure market access first and regional exports second, turning India, Indonesia and Thailand into supply bases for other markets.

What Chinese OEMs did

Four Chinese OEMs build in India. SANY's Chakan plant has absorbed more than INR 1,000 crore since 2007 and exported about 1,200 machines in 2024.21,22,98 XCMG has manufactured in Chennai since 2021, with capacity for 8,500 machines a year; Zoomlion opened an INR 500 crore plant at Khed City in May 2024; and LiuGong inaugurated an INR 272 crore expansion at Pithampur in July 2026.25–27

Indonesia has drawn the most recent investment. SANY announced a US$150m second phase at Karawang in 2023. LiuGong broke ground there in July 2026 on a US$317m plant to make 5,000 electric machines a year, and XCMG started making electric loaders at Weda Bay the same month.124,28–30 Thailand is the base for material handling: Hangcha opened a plant in Chonburi in June 2026, and Heli broke ground in Rayong in November 2025 on a joint venture to make 10,000 forklifts and 10,000 battery sets a year.31,32

Why build locally

  • Market access. In Indonesia a local plant earns a 25% credit towards the local-content score that governs public purchasing (Chapter 8).33
  • Insurance against tariffs and trade remedies. Under the US forced-labour Section 301 action, goods from India and Indonesia pay 10% against 12.5% for goods from China, though Thailand's rate matches China's.67 Plants outside China also sit beyond duties aimed at Chinese-made goods, such as the UK's excavator duties of 18.81–40.08% and the EU's anti-dumping case on mobile cranes.43,125
  • Proximity to customers. XCMG's Weda Bay plant sits in the industrial park where its first customer, Tsingshan, operates.30 Nearly 80% of XCMG's Indonesian staff are local.30
Case: the host country as an export base

LiuGong describes Karawang as its production base for Asia-Pacific before it expands exports to North America and Europe.28,29 Across India's whole industry, construction-equipment exports rose 31.5% to 17,394 units in FY26 while domestic sales fell, which the manufacturers' association linked to Indian machines meeting developed-market emission standards.95

How incumbents have responded

Most incumbents already own plants in the region. Volvo CE will launch localised wheel loaders in India in 2027.101 The open question is whether incumbents use their plants as export hubs as deliberately as Chinese entrants plan to.

Where it stops working

Plant economics depend on trade policy. The US investigation into excess capacity covers all five markets in this paper and could reach plants in the region (Chapter 15).65

What transfers

  • Global incumbents and Chinese OEMs should score plant decisions on export value, tariff protection and access to public purchasing, not home demand alone.
  • Indian and ASEAN champions can offer contract manufacturing and joint-venture capacity to foreign OEMs looking for a local base.
  • Policymakers can tie incentives to exports and local suppliers.

Chapter 8. Learning 5: Turn local-content and investment rules into an advantage

In briefChinese OEMs have used local-content and investment rules as sales tools that open public purchasing and partnerships. Many incumbents still treat the same rules as a compliance burden.

Indonesia now rewards investment itself

Indonesia's revised local-content rules, Permenperin 35/2025, give a 25% credit towards a product's TKDN score for investing and building a factory in Indonesia, a further 10% for employing local workers and 15% for good management. Where a domestic product exceeds 40%, government buyers must use it and may not import.33 The e-catalogue shows why this matters: Komatsu's PC200-class listings mostly show 51.21%, Hitachi's ZX200 listings 39.35%, and no SANY or XCMG listing in the class shows a score.117 Incumbents still hold the advantage in public purchasing, and Chinese entrants will need local production to close it. LiuGong has committed to raising local components at Karawang.29 Since February 2026, US products have been exempt from the rules.61

India has moved from blocking Chinese capital to channelling it

Since 2020, investment from countries that share a land border with India has needed government approval.35 Press Note 2 (2026), approved on 10 March, relaxes this. Investors based elsewhere may enter automatically where beneficial ownership from those countries is non-controlling and no more than 10%, and proposals in capital goods, electronic components and some related sectors must be decided within 60 days. The condition is that majority ownership and control stay with resident Indians.34 Legal commentators note that the 60-day timeline may not be binding.35

Other rules point the other way. Since 2020, bidders from land-border countries have been barred from public tenders unless registered and cleared, with sector exemptions.126,127 In September 2026 India's government e-marketplace listed no Chinese brand in crawler excavators, backhoe loaders, forklifts or small mobile cranes.129 The crane duties were recommended but not imposed.37 The net effect is that Chinese capital can now enter India as a minority partner more easily than Chinese-made machines can win public tenders.

Case: Thailand's incentives with obligations

Thailand's EV 3.5 scheme offered subsidies of up to THB100,000 per vehicle, but required two vehicles built locally for each one imported, rising to three in 2027, with fines of about THB400,000 per car for shortfalls.46 Neta's failure under the scheme (Chapter 12) shows the risk of taking incentives without the capacity to meet their obligations.

Where it stops working

Rules now move in both directions. India's tender rules still keep Chinese machines out of public purchasing, and Indonesia's exemption for US goods changes the calculation only for US-brand OEMs.

What transfers

  • Global incumbents should model local-content thresholds as a route into public purchasing, not a cost; the first to qualify often sets the specification.
  • Indian and ASEAN champions can use Press Note 2 to take Chinese technology and minority capital while keeping control.
  • Policymakers will get more from these rules by engaging early with firms on both sides.

How the entry model worked: the crane case

India's anti-dumping investigation into Chinese cranes is the most detailed public account anywhere in the region of how Chinese OEMs won a market. Its final findings, issued on 19 September 2025, covered crawler cranes of 40–260 tonnes and truck cranes of 25–160 tonnes, with April 2023 to March 2024 as the period of investigation.14

  • Volume. Imports from China rose from 163 units in FY21 to 667, 949 and then 938. China supplied 99–100% of India's imports each year.
  • Injury. Indian demand grew almost fivefold (an index of 100 rising to 488), yet the Indian industry's market share fell from 100 to 17 on an index, its loss per unit grew almost tenfold, and its inventories quadrupled.
  • Mechanism. Chinese prices undercut Indian ones by only 0–10%. The damage came from a squeeze: Chinese landed prices sat below the Indian producer's cost of sales, its costs rose 19 index points while import prices fell 18, and related importers had credit of up to three years.
  • Outcome. The regulator rejected SANY's submission for missing appendices and XCMG's for misreporting its credit terms. It recommended five-year duties of 24.87% for two named Zoomlion companies and 52.03% for all others.14,111 The government did not impose them within the three months India's rules allow, and they were still not in force in August 2026.37,new Media reports say five Indian producers have stopped making these cranes.37

Compressors, pumps and robots

Policy is pulling in two directions

India is opening and protecting at once. What India teaches

The full playbook works in India: a beachhead in long-reach excavators and cranes, four local plants, credit through related companies and non-bank lenders, and steadily widening product breadth. In briefThailand shows that the playbook depends on the category. China won forklifts, cranes, compressors and pumps there but made no headway in excavators. Vietnam and Malaysia are following Indonesia's excavator path faster than their reputations suggest.

Thailand is a useful test because it has no commodity boom to complicate the picture, and Japanese firms have built there for decades. Where the Chinese playbook works in Thailand, it works without a boom; where it fails, the reason is likely to lie in the category or the customer.

Thailand: two outcomes

Chapter 13. Where the playbook breaks

In briefThe playbook breaks in six documented ways. Two show where incumbents hold; four show where Chinese entrants are exposed. Each has an early-warning sign, and each points to a reader who can act on it.

A paper about learning from Chinese OEMs would be incomplete without the cases where their approach has not worked. Each failure mode below gives what happens, the evidence, the sign to watch and the learning it limits.

Where incumbents hold

1. Buyers return where uptime mattersLimits Learning 3

Where machines work hard and their resale value matters, buyers come back to incumbents. Tata Hitachi's share of Indian mining excavators fell from about 50% to 20% as Chinese makers entered, then recovered to about 35%.42

Watch for incumbent share rising in mining and other high-utilisation segments.

2. A loyal installed base holdsLimits Learning 1

A large installed base with local production and dealers can keep a category even when neighbouring categories are lost. In Thai excavators Japan's share of imports rose from 39.8% to 50.8% while China's stayed at about 36%.38

Watch for Chinese share flat in a category while it rises in the categories around it.

Where Chinese entrants are exposed

3. High share invites trade casesLimits Learnings 2 and 3

Trade remedies follow high share, and they follow fastest where credit between related companies is hard for regulators to see through. India's regulator rejected SANY's and XCMG's submissions in the crane case and recommended duties of up to 52.03%, although the government did not impose them.14,37 Export success also triggers remedies elsewhere. The UK imposed duties of 18.81–40.08% on Chinese-made excavators until 2030; the EU has put combined duties of up to 66.7% on Chinese mobile access equipment and, in December 2025, opened an anti-dumping case on Chinese mobile cranes.43,96,125

Watch for near-total import share in a category, local producers exiting, and a petition filed.

4. Overseas acquisitions lose moneyLimits Learning 4

Buying abroad without the capability to run the business destroys value. Efort's revenue fell 32.12% in 2025; it booked a RMB154.8m impairment on its overseas system-integration business, lost RMB497m and has not made a profit in any year since 2016.44,45,157

Watch for impairments on acquired overseas units.

5. Dealer networks outgrow parts supplyLimits Learnings 1 and 2

A dealer network that grows faster than its parts supply and its parent's finances breaks quickly. After Neta's parent went bankrupt, its Thai dealers sought more than THB200m and some repairs waited up to ten months for parts (Chapter 12).46

Watch for dealer numbers rising faster than parts depots and technicians, and lengthening parts waits.

6. Price wars at home drain cashLimits all six learnings

Price wars in China weaken the capacity to invest abroad. Efort's robot revenue fell about 21.5% in 2025 and its robot gross margin about 6.5 points, which the company put down to intense price competition and its own aggressive bidding.44

Watch for falling domestic margins at the parent company.

Read together, the failure modes show where the contest is decided. Incumbents hold where a machine's value over its life matters more than its price. Entrants are exposed where they grow faster than their support, or where their share grows faster than host governments will accept. Both point to the same ground: parts, service, finance and residual value, which is where the actions in Chapter 14 concentrate.

What remains unproven

Incumbents' claim that Chinese machines have “no resale price” remains untested in public data.42 Until a credible dataset of used-equipment prices by brand exists, it is best treated as a claim. Whichever side publishes such evidence first is likely to shape how lenders price Chinese machines.

Chapter 15. Outlook to 2030 and signposts

In briefThe next phase will be decided by finance, aftermarket services and trade policy rather than by new machines. Outcomes will differ by category, and eight signposts will show which way each is going.

No public forecast of brand shares to 2030 exists for these markets, so we do not attempt one. Instead we set out three paths, give our view by category, and name the signposts that would change it.

Three paths

  • Chinese share consolidates. Chinese captive finance and remanufacturing scale up in Indonesia, minority joint ventures multiply in India, and electric products extend Chinese leadership into the next generation.
  • Incumbents counter-attack. They aim their finance arms at first-time buyers, publish residual-value evidence, and win back share in mining and infrastructure, as Tata Hitachi has in Indian mining excavators.
  • Policy fragments the market. India starts imposing the trade remedies its regulator recommends, US tariffs reach plants in Southeast Asia, and Chinese plants in the region lose some of their export logic.

Conclusion

Conclusion: a playbook open to every firm

Chinese OEMs did not win share in India and Southeast Asia by being cheap. They entered through niches that incumbents served thinly, financed customers that banks would not, priced just low enough to matter, built plants that serve both the host market and its neighbours, used local rules as sales tools, and arrived with electric products as the technology changed. Their gains held through a coal peak in Indonesia and an equipment boom in India, which is why we judge the shift structural.

We asked which of these moves can be copied, by whom and how fast. Our answer is that most of them can be copied by almost any firm, and fastest by incumbents that already own finance arms, installed bases and local plants. The limits matter as much as the successes. Share has come back where uptime and resale value decide the purchase, trade remedies follow high share, and a channel that outgrows its parts supply fails quickly.

Three developments will decide the next phase, and each can be tracked: whether Chinese captive finance and remanufacturing scale up in Indonesia; whether India's investment rules produce minority Chinese partnerships in capital goods; and whether US trade actions change the export case for plants in the region. Chapter 15 sets out the signposts.

Appendix A. Method and definitions

A.1 Import shares

China's share of imports is the value of imports from China divided by the value of imports from the world, in US dollars, for the reporting country and product code. Data come from UN Comtrade through the World Bank's WITS portal, retrieved on 27 September 2026. The product codes are HS 842952 for 360° excavators; 842710 and 842720 for electric and other forklifts, summed; 842641 and 842649 for self-propelled cranes, summed; 841480 for air and gas compressors not elsewhere specified; and 841370 for centrifugal pumps not elsewhere specified. These codes are narrower than the four equipment families: they cover the main products in each, not every product. There is no trade measure for robots in this paper.

We compare 2019, the last full year before the pandemic and before most of the moves described here, with 2024, the latest full year most countries have reported. Vietnam has not reported 2024, so its latest year is 2023; where we use Vietnam's 2024 position, we use exporters' reports of their shipments to Vietnam (mirror data), which are valued differently and are not strictly comparable. Vietnam's 2019 excavator data show Japan and Korea with identical unit counts, so we check its trend against a 2020 base.

A.2 Definitions

  • Chinese OEM means an equipment maker headquartered in mainland China, counted by the brand it sells under. SDLG is a Chinese brand, which Volvo owned 70% of until 2025. AntOn is a Jungheinrich brand made for it in China by EP Equipment, so it counts as an incumbent's brand even though its imports count as Chinese in trade data. Machines that incumbents make in China, such as Caterpillar's Chinese output, likewise count as Chinese in trade data.
  • Equipment families. Construction and mining equipment (excavators, loaders, cranes and piling rigs); material handling (forklifts, and in the trade data self-propelled cranes); compressors, pumps and fluid power; and factory automation and robotics.
  • Market shares. This paper uses four kinds of share, which are not interchangeable: import shares by value (A.1); company-reported shares, such as United Tractors' Komatsu share, which the company bases on internal market research; Hexindo's reported share, which covers excavators above six tonnes up to 2023 and heavy-equipment sales in 2024 and 2025; and India's road registrations, which cover mostly wheeled machines.
  • Financial years. Indian figures marked FY run from April to March; FY26 is April 2025 to March 2026.
  • Currency. Figures are given in the currency reported. Headline local-currency figures carry a US-dollar equivalent at rates on 28 September 2026. Rates added at assembly

A.3 Strength of evidence

The grades for each learning by country rate the public evidence, not the size of the effect. Strong: regulator findings, filings or trade data show both the move and its effect. Moderate: the move is documented, but its effect rests on company claims or indirect data. Weak: there is little public evidence of the move.

A.4 Structural or cyclical

Chapter 3 applies five tests: whether the incumbent loses share in good years as well as bad; whether the shift began before the cyclical shock; whether it appears outside the cyclical segment; whether it holds in a control market without the cycle; and whether lost share has come back anywhere. A yes on any of the first three points to a structural shift; a no on the fourth points to an effect specific to the category; a yes on the fifth means the market is still contestable. Each market is then graded on a five-point scale: structural, likely structural, inconclusive, likely cyclical or cyclical.

A.5 Our calculations

  • Indonesian market size: United Tractors' Komatsu units divided by Komatsu's reported share, rounded to the nearest 100 units.
  • Combined Japanese share in Indonesia: Komatsu's share plus Hitachi's as reported by Hexindo. Because the two measure different things, the sum is indicative only.
  • China's share of Vietnam's 2024 excavator supply: China's reported exports to Vietnam as a share of China's, Japan's and Korea's combined.
  • Korea against Japan: each country's change in import share measured against its own 2019 share.
  • United Tractors' machine revenue: segment revenue less parts and service revenue, from the company's rounded figures.
  • Battery-pack prices in North America and Europe: BloombergNEF's China price raised by the 44% and 56% premiums it reports.

A.6 Sources and limits

We used only free, publicly verifiable sources: trade data, company filings and releases, regulators and governments, reputable media, and notes by law firms and research institutes. We used no paid databases, surveys or interviews, so buyers' views appear only as companies and dealers report them. Company claims, competitors' claims and our own calculations are labelled as such in the text. Import data record where a machine was shipped from, not whose badge it carries; they miss machines built locally and can be affected by re-exports and misclassification. Retail and e-catalogue prices are asking prices, not transaction prices. No free public dataset of used-equipment prices by brand exists in these markets, so claims about resale value remain untested.
