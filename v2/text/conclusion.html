Conclusion: a playbook open to every firm

Chinese equipment makers did not win share in India and Southeast Asia by being cheap. They entered through niches the big brands served thinly, financed customers that banks would not, priced just low enough to matter, built plants that serve both the host market and its neighbours, used local rules as sales tools, and arrived with electric products as the technology changed. Their gains held through a coal peak in Indonesia and an equipment boom in India, which is why we judge the shift structural.

We asked which of these moves can be copied, by whom and how fast, and which products they will reach next. Most of the moves can be copied by almost any firm, and fastest by established companies that already own finance arms, large customer bases and local plants. The next products are the machines factories buy, then robots, with standard plant equipment following more slowly.

How long established companies have depends on who their buyers are. Where buyers are contractors and fleets, the full Chinese approach has already arrived. Where they are factories, the window is a few years. For robots it runs to about the end of the decade, and for equipment bought off the shelf it is longest. The limits matter as much as the successes: buyers return where reliability decides the purchase, trade cases follow high share, and a dealer network that outgrows its parts supply fails quickly.

Five signs will show how fast the next phase moves, and Chapter 4 sets them out.
